Project definition · v0.3 · 24 Oct 2026 · Final-year B.Tech CSE capstone

Moraine

A glacial lake outburst flood twin for the Himalaya

Moraine maps glacial lakes from four decades of satellite records, replays tested flood simulations down real 3D valleys, and shows how many minutes of warning each town would get. It starts with the South Lhonak outburst in Sikkim (2023), then runs the same model, unchanged, on two floods in Nepal, Gongbatongsha (2016) and Thame (2024), and on Kedarnath (2013), where days of extreme rain did much of the damage.

§1

The problem

Glacial lakes grow as glaciers retreat. Many are held back only by moraine, the loose rock and ice a glacier leaves behind. If the moraine fails, the lake can empty within hours and send a flood down a narrow valley. Towns, bridges and hydropower dams downstream may get very little warning.

On 3–4 October 2023 this happened at South Lhonak lake in North Sikkim. Sattar et al. (2025) describe about 14.7 million m³ of frozen moraine sediment collapsing into the lake. It raised a wave of about 20 m that breached the moraine and released roughly 50 million m³ of water. At about 00:30 on 4 October the flood destroyed the 60 m Teesta-III dam at Chungthang. The lake had grown about twelvefold since 1975 [1–4]. Another study gives different volumes and a different trigger [5], so Moraine treats these figures as ranges.

India now runs a ₹150 crore National GLOF Risk Mitigation Programme in Sikkim, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Arunachal Pradesh. It targets 189–195 high-risk lakes. Its goals include a hazard assessment for each lake and automated early-warning stations [6–8].

The gap

Hazard assessments are built lake by lake in specialist desktop tools and usually end as reports and static maps. They are hard for planners to explore, and they rarely answer the practical question: where along the valley would one water-level sensor buy the most warning time?

§2

What Moraine does

Three modules share one 3D valley. Each answers one question.

Watch

Which lakes are growing, and how fast?

Every glacial lake in Sikkim and in the three test catchments above a size cut-off, outlined from satellite imagery each year since the late 1980s. An ML model fixes the cases a simple water index gets wrong: mountain shadow, ice cover, thin cloud and silty water. Each lake gets a growth curve, a volume estimate and a transparent risk score.

Lake outlines per year · area curves · volume estimates · risk score

Breach

If this lake fails, where does the water go?

Flood runs are computed ahead of time in Colab with a GPU shallow-water solver on 30 m terrain. The site plays them back: hindcasts of four real events and a set of what-if breaches for each priority lake. You scrub through time and switch scenarios. Nothing is simulated in the browser.

Depth and velocity over time · flooded extent · scenario set

Warn

Who gets how many minutes?

Arrival time, peak depth and peak speed at every town, bridge and dam. An optimizer chooses river sites for water-level sensors that give the most people the most warning, and compares them with simple placements.

Arrival isochrones · warning minutes per town · sensor sites

Two ways in

  • Story mode. Seven scroll chapters: the 2023 Sikkim event, the same pattern in Nepal, then Kedarnath 2013, where rain did much of the damage. Built for evaluators and the public (§9).
  • Explore. The working tool. Pick a valley and a lake, choose a breach scenario, scrub time, click a town for its numbers, and try sensor sites.

What is new here

  • One place to watch lakes, replay floods and plan warnings for real valleys. Existing work usually stops at a report.
  • One solver with settings fixed on the Sikkim flood, then tested unchanged on two Nepal floods and on Kedarnath, where it also takes in measured rain.
  • Warning time is the headline result: minutes per town, and the measured gain from each sensor site.
  • Every frame of water comes from a saved, tested simulation run and is labelled that way.
  • It runs on an ordinary laptop with no server.
§3

Scope and events

Pilot valley: Teesta, North Sikkim, from South Lhonak lake down to Chungthang.
Test valleys: Bhote Koshi (Tibet to Nepal), Thame / Dudh Koshi (Khumbu, Nepal) and Mandakini (Kedarnath, Uttarakhand).
Working projections: UTM zone 45N (EPSG:32645) for the first three valleys, 44N (EPSG:32644) for Kedarnath.

Must ship

  • Watch: yearly lake outlines for Sikkim and the three test catchments, above a size cut-off set in P0, late 1980s to 2026.
  • Lake ML model and a water-index baseline, tested on a held-out region.
  • Shallow-water solver, able to add rain on the grid, that passes the verification ladder in §8.
  • Hindcasts of four events: South Lhonak 2023, Gongbatongsha 2016, Thame 2024 and Kedarnath 2013. Kedarnath runs twice, with the lake alone and with measured rain added.
  • A 9-run scenario set for South Lhonak and a 3-run set for each Nepal lake. Chorabari lake drained in 2013 and has not refilled, so Kedarnath is hindcast only.
  • Warn: arrival maps, exposure counts for buildings, bridges and dams, and the sensor optimizer, for all four valleys.
  • Story mode (7 chapters), a valley switcher and the Explore workspace.
  • Static site that runs offline, with a provenance manifest.

Stretch

  • Rasuwa 2025, Tibet to Nepal: a transboundary warning case, once published studies settle the trigger.
  • Run the Sikkim flood past Chungthang to Singtam and Rangpo.
  • Scenario sets for three more Sikkim lakes.
  • Watchlist across all four Indian programme states.
  • Declassified 1970s spy-satellite film of South Lhonak in Chapter II.
  • A neural surrogate that predicts arrival times without running the solver.
  • Sensitivity test with a second elevation model.

Out of scope

  • Live flood simulation in the browser.
  • Official warnings, alerts or evacuation advice.
  • Debris-flow physics. Moraine models clean water and says so on screen.
  • Snowmelt modelling. Kedarnath adds measured rain only.
  • Building-level damage or water-depth claims.
  • Field visits or local gauge data. Every event uses public satellite data and published studies.
  • Accounts, login, server or database.

Events

Settings are tuned on the Sikkim pilot only. The other three events are the test set: the same solver runs on them unchanged, like a held-out split for the physics. Kedarnath is the hardest test. Chorabari lake was small, and a week of extreme rain did much of the damage [20], so Kedarnath runs twice: once with the lake alone and once with measured rain added on the grid. The gap between the two runs shows how much a lake-only model would miss. Chamoli 2021 stays out because it began as a rock-and-ice avalanche, not a lake outburst.

§7

Render budget

Your question

If the 3D view takes 60% of a 1920×1080 screen, can it render at that smaller size? Yes. There are two separate dials.

  1. Screen dial. The canvas is drawn at its own size on screen. 60% of the width at full height is 1152 × 1080, about 1.24 million pixels, 40% fewer than full screen. When the frame rate dips, the scene renders at 60–100% of that and upscales. On a laptop set to 125% Windows scaling, capping the pixel ratio at 1 works out to an 80% render scale.
  2. Data dial. How much terrain and flood detail to load depends on how much ground each pixel covers. A 40 km wide shot on 1152 pixels is about 35 m per pixel, so 30 m data is just right. Wider shots load a coarser level. Close-ups go finer than the data, so they get procedural detail.

The simulation grid is a third choice, separate from both. It is set in Colab for accuracy and reaches the browser only as file size.

§8

Science and validation

The solver is checked from the simplest exact case up to the real events before any of its output reaches the site.

  1. Ritter dam break (1892)Exact solution for a dam failing onto a dry bed. Check the front position and the depth profile. Passed in P0: within 0.86% at 5 m cells, the error halving as the cells halve, and also across the grid at 45° and down a 30% slope.
  2. Thacker's sloshing bowl (1981)Exact solution for water sloshing round a bowl, its shoreline climbing and falling the sides [26]. Checks moving wet/dry edges. Passed in P0: 0.19% depth error after one full orbit, wet area overlapping the exact one by 0.991.
  3. Rain on a closed basinRain falling on a flat, sealed basin must raise the water by exactly the rain total. Checks that rain added on the grid neither creates nor loses water.
  4. Malpasset dam failure, France, 1959The standard real-terrain benchmark, with field-measured arrival times and peak water levels. Brodtkorb et al. reproduced it with a GPU solver [9].
  5. Event hindcasts: Sikkim 2023, then Nepal 2016 and 2024, and Kedarnath 2013Settings are tuned on South Lhonak only, then frozen; the other three run unchanged. Each is scored against its own record: arrival at Chungthang around 00:30, the seismometer timing of the 2016 flood front [12], the 2024 breach time and damage in Thame [16], and the lake drain time and debris footprint at Kedarnath, with and without rain. Flooded extent is scored against post-event imagery (IoU).
  6. Scenario setBreach volume and duration varied across the published range of estimates.

Solver: the second-order Kurganov–Petrova scheme [10], written in PyTorch. It keeps water depth from going negative at wet/dry fronts and keeps a lake at rest still, islands and shorelines included, which matters on steep Himalayan slopes.

P0 findings, both fixed: where a shoreline crosses a cell, the 2007 scheme tilts that cell's water surface, and a still lake with islands started moving at about 2 m/s. Such cells now get a flat surface at the level that holds their water, after Bollermann et al. [25], and the lake stays still to 5×10⁻¹⁴ m/s. Moving water keeps the original surface, so the sloshing-bowl test scores the same as before. Separately, face velocities are now built from cell velocities, which stopped a 1 mm film running ahead of the flood front faster than physics allows.

Lake mapping

P0 finding: Landsat's own cloud mask labels dark lake water as cloud shadow, so the pipeline uses per-pixel percentile composites of each autumn instead of cloud masks. On South Lhonak this matches ISRO's pre-outburst area within 1% (1.668 against 1.674 km²).

Baseline: a water-index threshold (NDWI/MNDWI) with a slope mask. Model: a small U-Net on Sentinel-2 bands plus slope. It trains on other Himalayan regions and is tested on Sikkim, so no test lake leaks into training. Report IoU and area error per lake, plus a hard-case set: mountain shadow, frozen lakes, thin cloud and silty water.

Ranking backtest

Score every lake using only data from before each event (up to 2015, 2022 and 2023), then check where Gongbatongsha, South Lhonak and Thyanbo land. Chorabari is too small for satellites to measure, so it is left out. Would Moraine have flagged them? Small lakes under unstable slopes are the likely blind spot, and the backtest shows whether the score misses them.

Warning placement

Compare the optimizer's sensor sites with two simple placements: one sensor at the lake outlet, and sensors spaced evenly along the river. Report warning minutes gained per town, weighted by population.

Labels on every layer

ObservedSatellite images and the outlines measured from them.
ModelledSimulation output. It is a clean-water approximation: arrival time and extent are claimed, depth is indicative.
IllustrativeVisual effects the model doesn't compute, such as spray and foam density.

Open decisions

  • Team. Who owns the pipeline, solver, website and report? This plan assumes a small team using AI-assisted coding with weekly check-ins.
  • College calendar. Review dates, so the three gates line up with them.
  • Name. Moraine is a working title.
  • Watchlist reach. Sikkim only (MVP) or all four programme states (stretch).
  • Host. Vercel, GitHub Pages or Cloudflare Pages. All are static and free.
  • Accounts. A Google Cloud project registered for non-commercial Earth Engine use, plus Colab and Kaggle accounts.
